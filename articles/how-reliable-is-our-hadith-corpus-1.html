Response to Al-Islaah · No. 01

How Reliable Is the Imāmī Ḥadīth Corpus?

A comprehensive source-critical response: fabrication, the Yūnus report, the Four Books, Qurʾānic criteria, early safeguards and the methodological diversity of Imāmī scholarship.

Editorial draft30 October 2026 · Revised edition•37 min read•Ḥadīth Criticism

Editorial status: Extended research draft. All translated extracts are working translations unless otherwise noted. The article does not claim to offer an exhaustive critical edition of the manuscripts or final gradings of every individual report.

Abstract

The question is not whether fabricated material entered early Imāmī transmission: the sources explicitly testify that it did. The question is what follows from this fact for surviving individual reports, particular written recensions, and the wider corpus. An Al-Islaah article discussing Sayyid Kamāl al-Ḥaydarī argues that extremist infiltrators inserted fabricated traditions into early companions’ books; that the classical compilers failed to filter such material adequately; and that large portions of later collections are consequently untrustworthy. It emphasises a well-known report from Yūnus b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān and Qurʾānic scrutiny as the answer. These concerns demand a serious response, not an appeal to the sanctity of a book title. Yet an adequately demonstrated conclusion must distinguish reports shown to be fabricated from reports lacking a sound isnād, contaminated manuscript witnesses from independently transmitted recensions, and a report's potential authenticity from its legal authority. This study accepts the seriousness of early fabrication while examining how Imāmī scholars themselves identified, transmitted, rejected and contextualised reports. Its central conclusion is narrower, but stronger, than an unconditional defence of the corpus: documented fabrication establishes the necessity of criticism, not the demonstrated falsehood of every report exposed to a potentially compromised transmission environment. 1

1. What exactly does Al-Islaah claim?

A rebuttal begins by representing its opponent accurately. The Al-Islaah article by Syed Ali Hur Kamoonpuri presents a video attributed to al-Ḥaydarī, describing infiltration of early companions’ notebooks by al-Mughīrah b. Saʿīd, Abū al-Khaṭṭāb and their associates. It then advances several connected propositions: that the approximately four hundred early uṣūl were foundational to later compilations; that the compilers, notably al-Kulaynī and al-Ṣadūq, could have reproduced unreliable material from books of uncertain provenance; that a majority of Biḥār al-Anwār is fabricated; and that teachings attributed to the Imams require serious comparison with the Qurʾān and established Sunnah. The author also groups particular supernatural assertions among the ideas associated with the ghulāt. 1

These propositions must be disaggregated. Evidence that a particular extremist fabricated traditions does not by itself establish that every book available to that extremist was altered. Evidence that a compiler included a weak report does not, by itself, establish either that he knew it to be forged or that he authenticated it under modern technical standards. Likewise, evidence that many reports lack independently defensible chains does not provide the number of reports deliberately invented. The article repeatedly moves between these categories. The burden is on both sides to show how the transitions are justified.

There is a separate attribution issue. The embedded video, the Al-Islaah author's explanation, and al-Ḥaydarī's other lectures are not identical witnesses. One cannot automatically treat a statement recorded in one lecture as the verbatim language of another. Here we cite a publicly available official lecture transcript as direct evidence for al-Ḥaydarī's expressed methodological qualifications, not as a substitute for a full transcription of the embedded video. 2

2. Al-Ḥaydarī's own qualification: kathīr is not akthar

In his official lecture Min ahamm asbāb ʿadam al-thiqah bi-l-mawrūth al-riwāʾī (1), al-Ḥaydarī rejects the allegation that he dismisses the inherited narrated heritage outright. He maintains the possibility of accepting reports under conditions that are not exhausted by inspecting a chain of names. He also makes a significant quantitative correction:

كثير لا ليس أكثر هذا كذب علي

“Many—no, not most. That has been falsely attributed to me.”

Al-Ḥaydarī, official lecture transcript; translation by The Imāmī Review. 2

He refers to multiple conditions for acceptance and to his work Mīzān Taṣḥīḥ al-Mawrūth al-Riwāʾī. The distinction between “many” and “most” is not a minor rhetorical detail. “Many false reports exist” is compatible with several radically different possible proportions; “most of the corpus is forged” asserts a specific majority. Neither the authenticity of one warning report nor the discovery of several exposed forgeries provides a statistical estimate without a defensible denominator, classification procedure and sample.

That does not mean that al-Ḥaydarī’s suspicions should be ignored. His criticism draws attention to the methodological gap between reliable narrators and reliable statements, and to the historical effects of sectarian conflict. The strongest response must meet that concern with evidence, rather than simply reply that al-Kulaynī was a revered compiler.

3. The Yūnus report: source and transmitted structure

The core report occurs in Ikhtiyār Maʿrifat al-Rijāl, the abridged recension of al-Kashshī's rijāl work preserved by Shaykh al-Ṭūsī. In the Āl al-Bayt edition consulted here it is report 401, vol. 2, pp. 489–490. The outer chain is: Muḥammad b. Qūlawayh and al-Ḥusayn b. al-Ḥasan b. Bundār al-Qummī → Saʿd b. ʿAbd Allāh → Muḥammad b. ʿĪsā b. ʿUbayd → Yūnus b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān. The wording then relates Yūnus's response to an interlocutor; within that response, Yūnus transmits a statement he heard through Hishām b. al-Ḥakam from Imam al-Ṣādiq, before narrating his own encounter with Imam al-Riḍā. Those are two linked but distinct portions of the account. 3

The first part warns believers not to accept a falsely attributed narration merely because a claimant places it in an Imam's mouth. It recalls that al-Mughīrah inserted material into companions' books. Its criterion contains an often neglected second limb:

أَوْ تَجِدُونَ مَعَهُ شَاهِداً مِنْ أَحَادِيثِنَا الْمُتَقَدِّمَةِ

“Or you find a supporting witness for it among our earlier reports.”

*Ikhtiyār Maʿrifat al-Rijāl*, report 401, vol. 2, p. 489. 3

Yūnus next describes obtaining books and narrations in Iraq and showing them to Imam al-Riḍā, who rejected numerous reports as falsely attributed to Imam al-Ṣādiq and identified the continuing activity of Abū al-Khaṭṭāb's followers. In the following report (402), transmitted through the Yūnus–Hishām route, an additional description of al-Mughīrah's method appears. This is evidence for a concrete kind of book interpolation, not merely an abstract possibility of mistaken memory. 4

We should take the report at its actual strength: it speaks against assuming that a manuscript bearing a companion's name must contain exclusively that companion's accurately preserved narrations. It also supports comparing reports with the Qurʾān, established Sunnah and earlier independently credible traditions. It does not provide a comprehensive catalogue of every recension, indicate what fraction of surviving books was affected or identify which later quotations descend from contaminated copies.

4. Is the chain reliable? A qualification more precise than “ṣaḥīḥ”

An adequate discussion must assess the chain rather than rest entirely on its theological usefulness. The outer transmission includes well-known figures in the Qummī and Kufan transmission network. Saʿd b. ʿAbd Allāh and Yūnus are prominent transmitters. Muḥammad b. ʿĪsā b. ʿUbayd is especially important because some early assessments of his transmissions, including those linked to certain isolated paths from Yūnus, were contested. We cannot simply conceal this complication while invoking later approvals of the report. 5

Importantly, Sayyid Muḥammad Saʿīd al-Ḥakīm, in al-Muḥkam fī Uṣūl al-Fiqh, vol. 3, p. 213, explicitly describes al-Kashshī's transmission through Muḥammad b. ʿĪsā from Yūnus as possessing a sound chain. Thus there is identifiable contemporary Imāmī support for treating this testimony seriously. This does not turn the report into a mutawātir statement, nor establish every detail of the separate Hishām-to-Imam segment independently; it gives us grounds to treat the transmitted account as significant evidence. 6

There is a useful lesson in the apparent controversy concerning Muḥammad b. ʿĪsā. Al-Najāshī records objections to limited categories of his transmission, and also transmits Ibn Nūḥ's disagreement with an exclusion involving Muḥammad b. ʿĪsā. To judge this report responsibly one should identify whether the historical objection concerns the narrator in general, a particular disconnected route, or an individual book-copy relationship. A verdict on a name isolated from its specific transmission context is a poor substitute for rijāl. 5

Our methodological position is therefore candid: the Yūnus narration deserves serious evidentiary weight and cannot be brushed away as “fabricated,” but neither should one pretend that its chain status automatically resolves the entire sociological history of early ḥadīth transmission.

5. What does it prove—and what remains unproved?

Suppose one grants the report's authenticity and its historical content in full. We then know that some early books were penetrated by forgers, and that some reports attributed to the Imams were not theirs. Three further issues remain open. Scope: Which books and which portions of them were compromised? Transmission: Were the altered versions those later copied or cited by particular compilers? Independence: Did later parallel citations reflect independent textual witnesses, or did they all ultimately reproduce the same altered archetype?

This is not mere semantic hair-splitting. Imagine a book existing in five independently transmitted copies; one is altered. A quotation drawn from an independently preserved version need not inherit that alteration. Conversely, if the corrupt version became the sole archetype copied by a later generation, multiple later manuscripts could falsely appear to corroborate it. Both models are possible. The historical investigation must distinguish them; the warning report alone cannot decide which happened for every book.

A second distinction concerns ʿilm ijmālī: general knowledge that some unidentified reports within a set are erroneous. Its effects depend on the delimitation of the set, whether the potentially problematic cases are effectively dispersed or concentrated, and how other evidence resolves individual cases. It is too crude to turn knowledge that “some reports are false” into a conclusion that “this particular report is false,” without an additional argument.

This is not an attempt to remove the practical sting of the Yūnus narration. Rather, it locates the necessary next step: reconstruction of the written transmission and assessment of each report's context. A researcher who has proven only that contamination occurred has not yet proven which later witnesses transmitted it.

6. What al-Ṣadr actually argues about contaminated books

Al-Islaah invokes Sayyid Muḥammad Bāqir al-Ṣadr to press the difficulty posed by infiltration. His discussion in Mabāḥith al-Uṣūl, as reported by Sayyid Kāẓim al-Ḥāʾirī, is more subtle than a quotation stopping at the problem of general uncertainty. In the relevant discussion he explicitly considers the consequence of book interpolation and an ʿilm ijmālī about fabricated transmissions. He then proposes a reply based on the existence—or at least serious possibility—of several independent copies and separate lines of circulation. 7

He writes, in the passage beginning with al-makhlaṣ min hādhā al-ʿilm al-ijmālī, that the boundaries of insertion are not known and that the companions' books circulated in multiple copies; the forger would not ordinarily gain control of every copy. Consequently, one cannot assume, solely from the known insertion incidents, that the particular copies which reached the three major compilers were the altered ones. This is al-Ṣadr's proposed answer, not a demonstration that every surviving chain is safe. 7

Two objections deserve acknowledgement. First, the claim of multiple independently preserved copies needs specific evidence for specific books. Second, even where several copies existed, later recensions might descend from one common contaminated exemplar. An inference from the ordinary difficulty of altering all copies is useful but defeasible. Nonetheless, citing al-Ṣadr's objection while not acknowledging his reply would fundamentally distort the shape of his analysis.

المخلص من هذا العلم الإجمالي أنّ حدود هذا الوضع والدسّ غير معلومة

“The escape from this general knowledge is that the limits of this fabrication and insertion are unknown.”

Al-Ṣadr as reported by al-Ḥāʾirī, *Mabāḥith al-Uṣūl*, p. 393. 7

A fair research article should preserve both sides: the sceptic is entitled to demand proof of textual independence; the defender is entitled to challenge the unproved assumption that all surviving lines of transmission descended from the forged copy. The question then becomes empirical and textual, not merely polemical.

7. Were the “four hundred Uṣūl” a single compromised archive?

The expression al-uṣūl al-arbaʿumiʾah—the four hundred uṣūl—is familiar in Imāmī bibliographic discussions. But it should not be made to perform more historical work than the evidence permits. “Four hundred” is a traditional collective description; it does not identify one fixed, fully recoverable shelf of four hundred uniform books, nor establish that every report in later compilations was copied directly from those exact notebooks. Later collections drew on multiple books, intermediaries, recorded oral transmissions, and existing chains of transmission.

For the purposes of provenance, at least five things must be kept separate: the original author attributed to a book; a book with a similar or identical title known to a later scholar; the individual copy he possessed; the line by which he transmitted it; and any subsequent alterations of that copy. To say “al-Kulaynī used earlier books” establishes none of those five dimensions by itself.

The traditional biographical works themselves preserve useful information. Al-Najāshī's Fihrist, often called Rijāl al-Najāshī, is an important source for the titles of authored books and routes to them. Al-Ṭūsī's Fihrist and Rijāl provide additional routes and identifications. Comparing these with later citations can sometimes reveal a common book-based origin or a lost intermediary. But those catalogues are not complete manuscript stemmata, and a listed path must not be assumed to authenticate every later recension. 8

Thus the book-provenance approach strengthens rather than negates the warning from Yūnus. If books could be corrupted, the responsible answer is to investigate which book, which copy, and which transmission line. A statement about the general vulnerability of early notebooks cannot replace this work.

8. A documented early safeguard: Ibn al-Walīd's exclusions

Here we can move from speculation to concrete historical evidence. In the biography of Muḥammad b. Aḥmad b. Yaḥyā al-Ashʿarī, al-Najāshī reports that he was trustworthy as a transmitter, but also related material from weak transmitters and transmitted mursal reports. His large work Nawādir al-Ḥikmah became the subject of selective exclusions by the Qummī scholar Muḥammad b. al-Ḥasan b. al-Walīd. The exclusion list covers narrations through named transmitters and certain modes of anonymous or disconnected reporting. 9

Al-Najāshī further records that Shaykh al-Ṣadūq followed many of these exclusions, while Ibn Nūḥ contested one concerning Muḥammad b. ʿĪsā b. ʿUbayd. This matters precisely because the persons involved did not simply pronounce a whole famous compilation authentic or inauthentic. They examined particular routes and disputed the consequences of particular narrators or disconnected chains. 9

كان ثقة في الحديث، إلا أنّ أصحابنا قالوا: كان يروي عن الضعفاء ويعتمد المراسيل

“He was reliable in transmitting ḥadīth, but our companions said he transmitted from weak narrators and relied on disconnected reports.”

Al-Najāshī, *Fihrist*, p. 348. 9

There are limits to this evidence. The very existence of an exclusion list confirms that unreliable material could enter recognised books. It also demonstrates actual early criticism rather than hypothetical safeguards. What it does not establish is that Ibn al-Walīd's judgement was always correct, that every unexcluded report was authentic, or that other source books were scrutinised equally well. The proper conclusion is neither “they filtered everything” nor “they filtered nothing.”

Nor is agreement about a particular book's circulation equivalent to universal agreement about its contents. Al-Najāshī's record helps document critical practices and their internal disputes; it cannot certify the entirety of the corpus for us.

9. Al-Kulaynī's preface: a goal of sound transmission, not an infallible guarantee

In the introduction to al-Kāfī, al-Kulaynī recounts a request for a comprehensive work permitting religious learning and practice through al-āthār al-ṣaḥīḥah—sound reports—from the truthful authorities. He discusses discrepancies among narrations and presents criteria attributed to the Imams for handling such discrepancies. The title al-Kāfī, together with the author's purpose, clearly expresses an intention to compile useful and authoritative religious knowledge. 10

But careful reading matters. The preface recounts what the correspondent requested, and the relationship between that aspiration and the actual contents must be argued; it is not logically equivalent to a modern claim that every chain and text in the collection is indisputably authentic. Al-Kulaynī's book contains material with diverse chains, and its inclusion of a report can serve purposes beyond its individual authentication under later rijāl standards.

There is also an important complication for those who use the Qurʾānic standard against al-Kulaynī: al-Kāfī itself preserves a chapter on following the Sunnah and seeking witnesses from the Book. It reports the maxim that the Qurʾān must be used to assess transmitted material, and records disagreement between trustworthy and untrustworthy sources. That does not remove problematic reports from the work, but it demonstrates that the problem of contradictory attribution was part of the tradition al-Kulaynī was collecting. 11

A contemporary critic may still find individual reports unacceptable by isnād, content, or provenance. Such criticism is entirely consistent with taking al-Kulaynī's intellectual project seriously. Respect for a compiler should neither preclude criticism nor be confused with an unconditional certificate attached to every page.

10. Al-Ṣadūq's more explicit selection claim—and its limits

Al-Ṣadūq states in the introduction to Man Lā Yaḥḍuruhu al-Faqīh that he did not intend to include everything he had transmitted. Rather, he included what he issued legal opinions upon and judged authentic, and he names recognised source books used to prepare the collection. The explicitness of this claim is historically significant: it documents authorial selection and a relationship to recognised written sources. 12

بل قصدت إلى إيراد ما أفتي به وأحكم بصحته

“Rather, I intended to include what I issue rulings upon and judge to be sound.”

Al-Ṣadūq, *Man Lā Yaḥḍuruhu al-Faqīh*, introduction. 12

Yet it raises rather than closes an epistemological question: should al-Ṣadūq's personal authentication establish the report's authority for a later scholar with different criteria? Al-Khūʾī's treatment of the Four Books rejects that inference as a universal rule. A scholar's statement “I regard this as authoritative” can be evidence about his method, but it is not the same as independent proof that every transmission satisfies another investigator's standards. 13

Furthermore, the Nawādir al-Ḥikmah exclusions warn against assuming that a source's fame makes all its routes sound. The tradition of al-Ṣadūq and Ibn al-Walīd is a record of transmission and selective doubt. It is not adequately represented by either the claim that they accepted everything, or the claim that they made no meaningful effort to exclude material.

11. Al-Murtaḍā and al-Ṭūsī: one school, genuine disagreement

It would be methodologically misleading to present a single unanimous “classical Imāmī” theory of khabar al-wāḥid. Sayyid al-Murtaḍā argued that an isolated report does not, by itself, generate knowledge and denied its legal authority without the relevant decisive basis. His work does not logically entail that every solitary report is a lie; it distinguishes the truth-value of a proposition from the authority one may assign it in law or doctrine. 14

Shaykh al-Ṭūsī defended acting on reports under specified conditions. In al-ʿUddah, his discussion of transmissions by non-Imāmī transmitters, such as certain Wāqifī and Faṭḥī authorities, explicitly considers their trustworthiness, the absence of opposing accepted material and whether the community had acted on their reports. He also differentiates narrations attributable to extremists during a reliable period from those transmitted after their deviation. This is a historically important example of context-sensitive transmission criticism. 15

وجب أيضاً العمل به إذا كان متحرّجاً في روايته موثوقاً في أمانته

“It must also be acted upon if he is scrupulous in his transmission and trustworthy in his integrity.”

Al-Ṭūsī, *al-ʿUddah*, vol. 1, discussion of transmitters. 15

Their disagreement is not a contest between concern for authenticity and indifference to authenticity. It concerns what epistemic and legal conditions justify acting on a report. Ṣidq (factual truth), ḥujjiyyah (probative authority), and qaṭʿ (certainty) must be kept apart. A trustworthy report can be legally probative while still being fallible; a report not legally probative can coincidentally be true.

Consequently, an article claiming to represent the Imāmiyya should acknowledge these rival starting points. Neither a severe Qurʾān-first method nor a more permissive trustworthy-transmitter method can simply be declared the uncontested doctrine of all early scholars.

12. Al-Ḥurr al-ʿĀmilī and the stronger defence of recognised collections

Among later scholars, al-Ḥurr al-ʿĀmilī represents a notably more optimistic assessment of the inherited written sources. The ninth benefit in the conclusion of Wasāʾil al-Shīʿa, vol. 30, begins around p. 249 in the Āl al-Bayt edition, and develops arguments for the soundness of material transmitted from recognised books. His discussion is closely connected with the preceding benefits on the books used and the routes by which he received them. 16

His case is not merely “an important scholar said it.” It appeals to a historical culture of recording, transmitting and checking material, together with corroborating circumstances. A modern critic must therefore engage those particular arguments: how independent are the supporting witnesses; what does a preserved route prove about a copy's text; and whether early use of a book amounts to endorsement of every narrative within it.

There are serious objections to universal authentication of this kind. Common ancestry can make many witnesses less independent than they appear, and the standards of an early compiler may differ from those of a later reader. Nonetheless, it would be equally simplistic to treat reliance on qarāʾin as equivalent to blind acceptance. The dispute is about whether the proposed corroboration actually warrants the certainty or confidence claimed.

The more moderate project is to distinguish the authority of a recognised literary source from the authority of a particular report it carries. Neither follows automatically from the other.

Al-Baḥrānī and the internal diversity of Akhbārī arguments

Shaykh Yūsuf al-Baḥrānī, in the second introduction to al-Ḥadāʾiq al-Nāḍirah, explicitly defends the validity of transmitted reports and criticises the later division into four technical categories. His treatment offers an instructive contrast with al-Khūʾī's more exacting approach. Al-Baḥrānī should not be made to speak for all Akhbārīs, however; his sustained attention to conflicting reports and the principles of preference demonstrates an elaborate legal methodology rather than an assumption that no report could ever be problematic. The opening volume itself devotes detailed discussion to taqiyyah, conflicting narrations, the evidentiary role of the Qurʾān, and other hermeneutical questions. 25

The critic of al-Baḥrānī should therefore ask which documentary premises support his confidence in the early collections and whether those premises establish the authenticity of every report or merely make the collection normally dependable in legal practice. The defender should answer the particular objections based on defective copies and unreliable original sources, rather than invoking a broad historical claim about an entire school. This is a real dispute about evidence and authority, not a contest between reverence and irreverence toward the Imams.

Muḥaddith al-Nūrī: collecting neglected material while defending source provenance

Muḥaddith Ḥusayn al-Nūrī's Mustadrak al-Wasāʾil represents a different project. Its preface identifies reports omitted by al-Ḥurr's Wasāʾil, including those found in older works inaccessible to him, books whose authorship had not been accepted by al-Ḥurr, and even passages in books he already possessed. Nūrī set out to collect, organise and add this material; his Khātimat al-Mustadrak then investigates the books used and their authors. 26

This is relevant to Al-Islaah precisely because later scholars did not confine themselves to the Four Books or a single closed archive. Nūrī's Mustadrak demonstrates both the survival or recovery of further textual witnesses and the necessity of examining their provenance. Yet it would be an error to argue that a book becomes sound merely because Nūrī found and defended it. The question remains whether his evidence establishes the attribution of that source, its transmitted recension, and each report he extracted.

A historian can appreciate Nūrī's recovery work without sharing every one of his authenticity judgements. Equally, one cannot infer from the presence of an unfamiliar early title that all its reports originated in a late forgery. The work calls for source-by-source verification, which is exactly what a generalised claim about universal early contamination leaves undone.

13. Al-Khūʾī: an indispensable corrective to universal authentication

Sayyid Abū al-Qāsim al-Khūʾī's discussion in the introduction to Muʿjam Rijāl al-Ḥadīth directly challenges the universal authentication of the Four Books. He evaluates the claims made about al-Kāfī, al-Faqīh, al-Tahdhīb, and al-Istibṣār and argues that their narrations must be investigated, rather than treated as uniformly certain in attribution. 13

His position is significant because it grants an essential part of the modern critical objection: inclusion in a respected compilation is not enough. He identifies problems of weak chains, uncertain routes and texts that resist acceptance. But his conclusion is not the elimination of the corpus. It is a reasoned return to evaluating the available evidence for individual narrations.

والمتحصّل أنّه لم تثبت صحّة جميع روايات الكافي

“The result is that the soundness of all narrations in al-Kāfī has not been established.”

Al-Khūʾī, introductory discussion to *Muʿjam Rijāl al-Ḥadīth*. 13

This positions al-Khūʾī differently from both a universal collection-authentication theory and a global suspicion that the whole corpus should be regarded as presumptively forged. The first denies too much room for criticism; the second often asserts a quantitative result without establishing it. Al-Khūʾī makes the evidential burden report-specific.

One should not romanticise a purely name-based approach either. Narrator reliability does not eliminate the possibility of a forged book presented to a trustworthy reader, a slipped intermediary, or a common source behind ostensibly separate chains. Where these risks are concrete, book-provenance and textual analysis supplement the individual rijāl entry.

14. Al-Sīstānī: Qurʾānic coherence and the history of transmission

A compelling response to the article should not portray Qurʾānic scrutiny as the exclusive possession of al-Ḥaydarī. In Qāʿidat Lā Ḍarar wa-Lā Ḍirār, p. 215, Sayyid ʿAlī al-Sīstānī interprets agreement or disagreement with the Qurʾān and established Sunnah, on his preferred reading, as involving their deeper orientation, identifiable through internal and external contextual indications. He does not confine agreement to a literal duplication of an existing verse. 17

التوافق أو التخالف الروحي بينه وبينهما على ما تشهد به قرائن داخلية وخارجية

“The underlying compatibility or incompatibility between it and them, as attested by internal and external indications.”

Al-Sīstānī, *Qāʿidat Lā Ḍarar*, p. 215. 17

This matters because many detailed legal and devotional teachings are not quoted verbatim in the Qurʾān. If “agreement” required an explicit matching Qurʾānic sentence, the use of ḥadīth to explain scriptural principles would become largely impossible. Conversely, it would be a mistake to use the existence of extra-Qurʾānic details as an excuse to overlook a narration that genuinely clashes with definitive Qurʾānic teaching.

His official office also describes expertise in establishing a narration's attribution as including rijāl, the identification of source books, recognition of interpolation, awareness of motives for fabrication, comparison of differing manuscripts, and separation of the original narration from later authorial commentary. That is directly pertinent to the contamination problem. 18

This is an example of a rigorous Imāmī approach in which the textual, bibliographic, historical and jurisprudential inquiries are not rivals but interacting parts of verification.

Al-Burūjerdī and the value of assembling parallel witnesses

The introduction to Jāmiʿ Aḥādīth al-Shīʿa, produced under Sayyid Ḥusayn al-Burūjerdī's supervision, sets out an editorial programme that includes gathering reports by subject, distinguishing parallel formulations and shared final transmitters, reproducing material on conflicting reports, and drawing attention to apparent confusions in chains and texts. It also mentions the risk of mistakenly taking al-Ṣadūq's legal judgement to be the wording of an original narration. Those are specific safeguards against misreading a compiled corpus. 27

The achievement is predominantly documentary and comparative: arranging evidence so that jurists can investigate it more effectively. It does not follow that every narration reproduced in Jāmiʿ is consequently authenticated, nor that the collection possesses a new manuscript for every report. Its usefulness is different: it enables researchers to see parallels and variants together rather than mistakenly treating each appearance in a later compilation as wholly independent evidence.

This also reveals an important historical development from the early uṣūl and jawāmiʿ, through the Four Books and al-Ḥurr's Wasāʾil, to Nūrī's supplementary collecting and al-Burūjerdī's reorganisation. Each project has its own goal. None of their titles or compilers' reputations substitutes for investigating a particular disputed report.

15. What do the “present it to the Qurʾān” narrations actually require?

The narrations directing believers to compare ḥadīth with the Qurʾān are not invented by modern reformers. Al-Kāfī includes examples in its chapter on following the Sunnah and scriptural witnesses. The report of Yūnus likewise appeals both to the Qurʾān and to corroborating earlier reports. The hard question is how broadly to define “agreement” and “contradiction.” 11

A method of explicit verse matching would treat a narration as acceptable only if its detailed proposition could be located in the Qurʾān. That is much stricter than non-contradiction with definitive teaching. A third possibility, developed in Imāmī uṣūl, is agreement with the Qurʾān's general moral, doctrinal and legal orientation. These should not be conflated. A report stating that two units of prayer are performed in a particular circumstance does not necessarily contradict the Qurʾān simply because the Qurʾān never directly specifies that circumstance.

Equally important is the classical distinction between tabāyun (irreconcilable opposition), takhṣīṣ (specification), and taqyīd (qualification). If the Qurʾān expresses a general rule and an independently authoritative report provides an instance-specific limitation, the question is whether that report can serve as an explanatory indicator, not whether any difference in wording proves forgery. Al-Ṣadr's recorded uṣūl discussions consider multiple kinds of Qurʾānic opposition and their different consequences. 19

The sceptical scholar may object that a fabricated tradition can be crafted to seem superficially consistent with the Qurʾān. Correct. Agreement with a broad Qurʾānic ideal is not sufficient evidence that the words were spoken by an Imam. A narrator can forge a pious statement. The Qurʾānic test is therefore partly negative—it can expose irreconcilable reports—but often needs the support of positive historical attribution evidence.

16. The difference between a weak report and a forged report

For analytical clarity, this study employs five separate judgements. Authenticated under a stated test: the report satisfies a particular chain-based or cumulative evidential criterion. Probatively usable: a jurist or theologian can rely on it under an articulated theory of ḥujjiyyah. Unverified: the presently available evidence is insufficient to establish attribution. Contradicted: evidence supports a genuine conflict with stronger established information. Fabricated: positive evidence indicates that the claimed attribution is false.

These categories overlap in some cases but are not interchangeable. An unreliable chain may preserve a genuine statement; a formally strong chain may transmit a corrupted text; multiple weak lines may contain genuine common information; and multiple weak lines copied from the same forged source may be worthless as corroboration. Reliability is not binary without specifying the question being asked.

This distinction is central to interpreting claims about large compilations. One cannot take the number of reports that fail a later technical ṣaḥīḥ standard and relabel that number as the proportion of deliberately forged reports. The terms represent different judgements, proven by different kinds of evidence.

This is where a methodological rebuttal can be stronger than a polemic: it need not invent a favourable percentage, and it should refuse an unfavourable percentage that has not been justified by an identified method.

17. Biḥār al-Anwār: a library of reports, not one uniformly graded collection

Biḥār al-Anwār was a vast project of collecting earlier material. Its scale and diversity of sources make a simplistic numerical verdict especially problematic. Later users of the collection may encounter direct quotations from recognised books, reports with uncertain or disputed ascriptions, fragmentary material, and versions preserved only through relatively late witnesses. Each demands a different kind of argument.

Shaykh Muḥammad Āṣif al-Muḥsinī provides a particularly useful corrective. In the opening of Mashraʿat Biḥār al-Anwār, vol. 1, p. 9, he states explicitly that most narrations gathered in Biḥār are not reliable by their chains. Yet he immediately distinguishes that result from saying those texts were all invented. He allows that cumulative convergence of reports can establish a shared meaning, and that independent indicators can establish reassurance of attribution in individual cases. He also warns of the danger of uncritically relying on unsound narrations. 20

أكثر الروايات المستخرجة والمنقولة في البحار غير معتبرة سنداً

“Most narrations extracted and transmitted in Biḥār are not probative on isnād grounds.”

Al-Muḥsinī, *Mashraʿat Biḥār al-Anwār*, vol. 1, p. 9. 20

In a note elsewhere he explains that neither the validity of a chain guarantees all aspects of the report's issuance, meaning and content, nor does a defective chain prove that the words were never spoken. This is a substantive methodological position, not a convenient escape from criticism. 21

The correct question about Biḥār is therefore not “Is it authentic or fabricated?” but “What is the source and status of this report as quoted here?” When the article claims that a majority of Biḥār is fabricated, it must offer something other than a count of reports that fail a chain criterion. Without that, the quantitative assertion remains unproved.

18. Ghuluw requires definition, not intuition

The Al-Islaah article associates extremist fabrication with extraordinary claims about the Imams. This is an important warning: historical ghulāt did invent teachings that erased the distinction between Creator and creature. But “extraordinary,” “unusual,” “miraculous,” and “theologically extreme” are not synonyms. To evaluate a report one must first define the prohibited doctrine.

Shaykh al-Ṣadūq condemns the ghulāt and mufawwiḍah. Shaykh al-Mufīd, while commenting on the creed of al-Ṣadūq, treats the attribution of divinity or prophecy to the Imams and the doctrine of independently delegated creation and sustenance as examples of crossing the theological boundary. That is a description of unacceptable metaphysical claims, not an argument that every extraordinary account is necessarily impossible. 22

الغلاة من المتظاهرين بالإسلام هم الذين نسبوا أمير المؤمنين والأئمة من ذريته إلى الألوهية والنبوة

“Extremists claiming Islam are those who attributed divinity or prophethood to the Commander of the Faithful and the Imams descended from him.”

Al-Mufīd, *Taṣḥīḥ al-Iʿtiqādāt*, discussion of ghuluw. 22

Al-Majlisī's discussion in Biḥār, vol. 25, pp. 346–347, distinguishes claims of inherently possessed divine qualities from claims of gifts or knowledge granted by God. He also cautions against condemning trustworthy transmitters merely because their reports include remarkable miracles. This caution must be read alongside his rejection of doctrines which make the Imams co-creators or independent providers of sustenance. 23

The Qurʾān itself reports extraordinary acts granted to created beings: Jesus acts by God's permission (3:49), and one possessing knowledge from the Book brings the throne to Solomon (27:40). These verses establish that a supernatural occurrence is not intrinsically a claim to divinity. They do not establish any particular later narration about the Twelve Imams. That still requires proper historical and doctrinal examination.

19. A necessary criticism of selective matn judgement

It is correct to subject a narration's matn to critical scrutiny. It is not correct to suppose that a modern reader's surprise or discomfort establishes forgery. Shaykh Jaʿfar al-Subḥānī, in Kulliyyāt fī ʿIlm al-Rijāl, p. 98, warns against making a critic's personal theological preferences the decisive criterion of authentication. His discussion also recalls al-Majlisī's warning about the loose use of ghuluw as a jarḥ against narrators. 24

This does not mean that theology cannot constrain an attribution. Definitively established tawḥīd matters. What it means is that the critic must identify the premise, its certainty and the exact point at which the report contradicts it. For example, “the Imam independently creates without God” is not equivalent to “God enables a miracle through an Imam.” But “God enabled it” should not become a formula that automatically authenticates every imaginable supernatural story.

The same restraint should apply in the other direction. A historically false report does not become reliable merely because it is phrased in a way that respects the correct doctrine of divine sovereignty. Genuine matn criticism and rigorous source criticism are complementary checks.

20. More independent transmissions are not always more independent evidence

Suppose five books quote the same striking report. It is tempting to call this fivefold corroboration. Yet if each book copied from a single intermediary, the five citations may count as one historical textual witness. A useful source-critical analysis therefore distinguishes multiple appearances from independent attestations. The researcher must look at shared wording, uncommon mistakes, identical omissions, common chains, book attributions and the dates of surviving witnesses.

A report in al-Kulaynī and a report in al-Ṣadūq may reflect two genuinely separate transmission paths, or a previously shared source. Neither possibility can be inferred solely from the compiler names. Likewise, a narration surviving in a later copy of a text quoted in Biḥār may represent the same recension Majlisī saw, or a different one. A printed edition cannot answer the question without an editorial account of the manuscripts.

This observation also qualifies al-Ṣadr's multiple-copy reply. A forger need not control every surviving page if one altered exemplar later becomes the ancestor of all surviving copies. Conversely, the mere existence of such a possibility does not establish that this happened in any particular case. Evidence about stemmatic relationships is required.

For this reason, our proposed editorial practice is to document earliest known attestation, independence, manuscript or edition witness, chain structure, and parallel readings before using a report as corroboration. Where that work is not possible, the conclusion should be explicitly provisional.

21. The strongest challenge to this response

A fair rebuttal must present its best opposing argument. The sceptic can reasonably say: if even trustworthy companions' books were vulnerable to planted material, and if the surviving record has significant gaps, then the chain of apparently trustworthy individuals may fail to authenticate the underlying written exemplar. The burden is on whoever wishes to use a report, not on whoever doubts it. Furthermore, repeated orthodox-sounding reports could have been shaped by the same sectarian pressures; agreement with the Qurʾān or an accepted doctrine does not prove their historical issuance.

That objection is powerful. We accept that thiqa status is not a magical guarantee, and that reliance on book provenance or a reputation for orthodoxy can become circular. We also accept that the scholar defending authenticity bears an evidential burden. Our disagreement is with expanding a legitimate warning about possibility and particular documented incidents into an unquantified declaration that most reports in one or every collection are positively fabricated.

The critic need not prove that every unverified narration is forged in order to decline to use it. Simply lacking adequate proof of attribution may suffice for non-use under that scholar's standards. This is an important concession: the rejection of sweeping forgery statistics does not mean every report remains practically admissible.

Conversely, the defender need not prove that every report is preserved in pristine form to use a specific report whose transmission is supported by genuinely adequate evidence. Responsible scholarship combines the sceptic's demand for proof with a careful separation of uncertainty, legal non-use and demonstrated false attribution.

22. A practical Imāmī protocol for investigating a disputed narration

The most constructive outcome is a procedure applicable to concrete disputes. We propose the following protocol, not as the uniquely binding Imāmī school, but as an evidence-led synthesis of widely recognised concerns:

  1. Define the claim. Is the question historical occurrence, doctrinal compatibility, literary attribution, or legal probativity? These can have different answers.
  2. Identify the earliest recoverable witness. Record the source title, author, edition, volume, page, report number and whether we possess the book itself or only a later quotation.
  3. Transcribe and collate. Compare the Arabic wording across editions and available manuscripts. Preserve relevant variants instead of silently harmonising them.
  4. Map the chains and books. Check rijāl, ṭabaqāt, encounter possibilities, missing intermediaries and the relationship between quoted narrations and written sources.
  5. Test independence. Ask whether apparently multiple chains descend from one person, one book, one corrupted edition or one circulating formula.
  6. Examine content. Test against definitive Qurʾānic principles, established Sunnah, chronology, language and demonstrable doctrine; distinguish contradiction from explanation or qualification.
  7. Compare Imāmī scholarly evaluations. Quote the actual judgement, explain the scholar's standard and distinguish his interpretation from evidence that a report is true or false.
  8. State a calibrated result. Examples include “strongly corroborated,” “probative on this jurist's standard,” “textually uncertain,” “weak chain,” “contradicts definite evidence,” or “positively forged.”

A report may need to be treated differently in fiqh from theology or historical writing, especially where a jurist accepts a particular kind of ḥujjiyyah in practical law without claiming historical certainty. This nuance is essential in any comparison between al-Murtaḍā, al-Ṭūsī, al-Ḥurr, al-Khūʾī and more contextual approaches.

23. Findings: what the evidence does—and does not—justify

The following judgements summarise the material examined here, subject to the limits of the identified witnesses:

Proposition Assessment
Extremists inserted false narrations into early Imāmī books. Strongly supported by the Yūnus report, treated as sound by identifiable later scholars.
The early Imāmī compilers possessed no critical safeguards at all. Contradicted by direct records of selection, exclusions and evaluation; their effectiveness remains case-specific.
Every one of the four hundred uṣūl was contaminated in the same way. Not established by the cited narration.
A sound outer chain automatically proves an unimpaired book-copy behind it. Not established; book provenance remains relevant.
The Four Books are uniformly authentic. Disputed in the Imāmī tradition; explicitly rejected by al-Khūʾī.
Every report not explicitly repeated in the Qurʾān is invalid. Not a necessary interpretation of the comparison reports; al-Sīstānī and others read agreement more broadly.
Most of Biḥār fails an exacting chain-based criterion. Al-Muḥsinī explicitly argues this.
Most of Biḥār is positively fabricated. This stronger statistical conclusion has not been demonstrated here.
Extraordinary reports necessarily constitute ghuluw. Incorrect without distinguishing independent divine prerogatives from purported God-given acts and assessing the report.

The resulting position is neither an uncritical Akhbārī defence of every transmitted statement nor the wholesale suspicion that follows from collapsing all uncertainty into forgery. It is an insistence that each level of conclusion must be justified by evidence appropriate to it.

Conclusion

The authors and transmitters of early Imāmī books operated in an environment in which fabricated traditions existed. The Yūnus report is an unusually important witness to the practice of inserting forged material into companions' books, and modern Imāmī scholarship is right to take it seriously. The proper response is not denial. It is historically careful examination of the books and reports whose attribution has become uncertain.

At the same time, the existence of forgery cannot itself demonstrate that any arbitrarily selected narration in a later collection is forged, or that a numerical majority of the corpus has been fabricated. The difference between these statements is not apologetic: it is basic historical reasoning. Early records of book-specific criticism, the competing accounts of khabar al-wāḥid, al-Ṣadr's discussion of multiple recensions, al-Khūʾī's refusal of universal collection-authentication, al-Sīstānī's wider approach to textual coherence and provenance, and al-Muḥsinī's distinction between weak chains and fabrication all show that the Imāmī tradition contains resources for a more exacting analysis than either blanket acceptance or blanket rejection.

The responsible question is therefore not “Can one trust all of our books?” but “What convergent historical, textual, isnādī and doctrinal evidence establishes the attribution of this particular report?” An answer to that question can be affirmative, negative or provisionally undecided. It need not be predetermined by a book's reputation or by a researcher’s polemical preferences.

The present paper is a researched draft, not a claim that every historical problem is settled. Especially important work remains in mapping the separate recensions of the uṣūl, checking the details of individual modern gradings and comparing manuscript witnesses for disputed reports. Those are suitable subjects for independent follow-up studies rather than claims to have been completed within a single general response.
