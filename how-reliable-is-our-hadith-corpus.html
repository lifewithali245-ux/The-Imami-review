Response to Al-Islaah · No. 01

How Reliable Is Our Ḥadīth Corpus?

A close reading of Al-Islaah’s argument, the report of Yūnus b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, and the limits of its conclusions.

Editorial draft28 October 2026•12 min read•Ḥadīth Criticism

The question at issue

The Al-Islaah article “How reliable is our Hadith Corpus?”, discussing Sayyid Kamāl al-Ḥaydarī, rightly foregrounds a serious problem: early Imāmī books were targets of fabricated attribution. It is right to insist that a narration is not immune from criticism merely because it appears in a respected compilation.1

Yet a separate question remains unanswered by that observation alone. What follows about the later corpus from evidence that some early copies were interpolated? To move from the existence of fabrication to the unreliability of most transmitted reports, one must establish the relevant scale, routes of transmission and degree of textual dependence.

What al-Ḥaydarī actually says

Al-Ḥaydarī’s official lecture transcript is a necessary control on any secondary summary of his position. In it, he rejects the suggestion that he repudiates the inherited ḥadīth tradition as such and distinguishes kathīr (“many”) from akthar (“most”).2

كثير لا ليس أكثر هذا كذب علي

“Many, no—not most. That is a false attribution to me.”

Al-Ḥaydarī · Official lecture transcript · editorial translation

His substantive position remains highly critical: the isnād is not the only consideration, and the existence of interpolation requires collecting additional indicators of reliability. But attributing to him the claim that the majority of every collection is demonstrably invented goes beyond this explicit clarification.

The report of Yūnus: real evidence, limited inference

The account of Yūnus b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, preserved in al-Ṭūsī’s recension of Rijāl al-Kashshī, reports the insertion of false material into books associated with companions of Imams al-Bāqir and al-Ṣādiq (ع). The report is the central early text cited by the article and is worthy of serious scrutiny.3

لَا تَقْبَلُوا عَلَيْنَا حَدِيثاً إِلَّا مَا وَافَقَ الْقُرْآنَ وَالسُّنَّةَ أَوْ تَجِدُونَ مَعَهُ شَاهِداً مِنْ أَحَادِيثِنَا الْمُتَقَدِّمَةِ

“Do not accept a narration attributed to us unless it agrees with the Qurʾān and the Sunnah, or you find for it corroboration among our earlier narrations.”

Report of Yūnus · Rijāl al-Kashshī, no. 401 · editorial translation

The corroboration clause matters. The report is not only a warning against fabrication; it also identifies earlier transmitted material as a possible witness. It therefore supports investigation of qarāʾin rather than the automatic dismissal of an entire body of narrations.

What the report supports

Interpolation occurred; some attributed reports were false; verification must be serious.

What it does not quantify

How many surviving later reports were affected, or whether every manuscript line was compromised.

Compilation is not authentication

An encyclopaedic compiler can preserve a report without certifying it under every later technical standard. Equally, the existence of a report in a canonical collection is not proof of its authenticity. These are distinct propositions, and a careful rebuttal should affirm both.

Al-Kulaynī’s introduction to al-Kāfī expressly addresses conflict between narrations; his collection includes a chapter on following the Sunnah and the evidences of the Qurʾān. That does not establish the accuracy of every individual report, but it does challenge the notion that the compiler had no critical conception of transmission.4

A productive criticism proceeds narration by narration, then also investigates the source book, its recension and its transmission history. Reliability attaches to evidence for an attribution, not to the prestige—or disrepute—of a book title alone.

The methodological distinction

Three judgements must remain separate: a report may be sound by a specified isnād standard; it may be supported through other independent evidence; or it may be positively demonstrated to be forged. Failing the first test is not, by itself, proof of the third.

Critical principle

“Not authenticated on the evidence presently available” is a narrower finding than “fabricated.” A scholarly response should not silently exchange one for the other.

This distinction is especially important with large compilations such as Biḥār al-Anwār, where reports appear with varying degrees of documentation and from source works of differing status.

A constructive Imāmī response

There is no need to deny that extremists forged narrations. Nor is there a need to assume that every unusual report is a forgery. The better procedure is cumulative and explicit:

  1. Identify the exact wording, edition and earlier textual witnesses.
  2. Assess the narrators, possible intermediary omissions and chronology of transmission.
  3. Investigate the provenance and competing recensions of the source book.
  4. Compare independent parallels, early citations and relevant Qurʾānic or established Sunnah evidence.
  5. State the conclusion at the strength the evidence warrants, identifying unresolved questions.

The resulting answer can accept the historical warning in Yūnus’s report while rejecting conclusions about the entire corpus for which the report itself provides no quantification.
